# Jonah

World English Bible text (public domain), with a date heading for each chapter.
See [How to read the date headings](README.md#how-to-read-the-date-headings).

<a id="date-of-writing"></a>

## Date of writing: 8th century BC (traditional) or c. 500–300 BC (most critical scholars)

Jonah is a story about a prophet, not a collection of oracles. Its hero is "Jonah the son of Amittai" (1:1), the prophet who foretold Jeroboam II's expansion (2 Kgs 14:25), so the story is set in the first half of the 8th century BC. When it was written is disputed: tradition takes it as an account from Jonah's own time, while most critical scholars date the book after the exile and read it as a didactic story.

| Date | Held by | Basis | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8th century BC, an account of real events | Conservative scholars (Dillard & Longman discuss the options); Jewish and Christian tradition | The prophet is a known historical figure of Jeroboam II's reign; Jesus refers to "the sign of Jonah" and to the men of Nineveh repenting (Matt 12:39–41; Luke 11:29–32); Assyria was weak and troubled in the first half of the 8th century, which might make its capital receptive. The Talmud lists Jonah among the Twelve, ascribed to the men of the Great Assembly as compilers, not authors. | [Dillard & Longman](../reference/sources.md#dillard-longman); [Talmud, Bava Batra, 14b–15a](../reference/sources.md#bava-batra) |
| c. 500–300 BC, a didactic story | Most critical scholars (Collins); Sasson discusses the evidence | Late Hebrew and Aramaic words and idioms (e.g. *ta'am* for a royal decree, 3:7); Nineveh "was" a great city (3:3), spoken of as long gone (it fell in 612 BC); a "king of Nineveh" (3:6) rather than "king of Assyria"; the city's vast size (3:3; 4:11); the psalm (2:2–9) built from phrases of the Psalms. Its theme, God's mercy to a foreign enemy, is often read as a response to the exclusivism of the Persian period. Sirach 49:10 (c. 180 BC) knows "the Twelve Prophets", which sets the lower limit. | [Collins, Hebrew Bible](../reference/sources.md#collins-intro); [Sasson 1990](../reference/sources.md#sasson-jonah); [Sirach, 49:10](../reference/sources.md#sirach) |

## Jonah 1

> **Date of events:** c. 780–753 BC (setting under Jeroboam II)
>
> Jonah son of Amittai is the prophet of 2 Kings 14:25, from Gath Hepher, who foretold Jeroboam II's recovery of Israel's borders, so the story is set in or near Jeroboam's reign (793–753 BC on Thiele's dates). Jonah flees from Joppa on a ship for Tarshish (1:3), and is thrown overboard in the storm. Many critical scholars read the date as the story's setting, not the time of writing.
>
> **Basis:**
> - [Jeroboam II, Uzziah and the Assyrian advance](../reference/anchors.md#eighth-century), c. 793–722 BC. Jeroboam II (793–753) and Uzziah (792–740) ruled in prosperity; Tiglath-pileser III (745–727) then pushed west, Israel and Aram attacked Judah (the Syro-Ephraimite war, 734–732) and Damascus fell in 732. The eclipse of 763 BC in the Assyrian eponym list fixes these dates.
>
> **Sources:** [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Cogan; Cogan & Tadmor](../reference/sources.md#cogan-kings); [Assyrian eponym list](../reference/sources.md#eponym-canon); [ANET](../reference/sources.md#anet).
>
> **Book written:** 8th century BC (traditional) or c. 500–300 BC (most critical scholars); see [the date of Jonah](#date-of-writing).

<sup>1</sup>Now the LORD’s[^1-1] word came to Jonah the son of Amittai, saying, <sup>2</sup>“Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and preach against it, for their wickedness has come up before me.”

<sup>3</sup>But Jonah rose up to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD. He went down to Joppa, and found a ship going to Tarshish; so he paid its fare, and went down into it, to go with them to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD.

<sup>4</sup>But the LORD sent out a great wind on the sea, and there was a mighty storm on the sea, so that the ship was likely to break up. <sup>5</sup>Then the mariners were afraid, and every man cried to his god. They threw the cargo that was in the ship into the sea to lighten the ship. But Jonah had gone down into the innermost parts of the ship and he was laying down, and was fast asleep. <sup>6</sup>So the ship master came to him, and said to him, “What do you mean, sleeper? Arise, call on your God![^1-2] Maybe your God[^1-3] will notice us, so that we won’t perish.”

<sup>7</sup>They all said to each other, “Come! Let’s cast lots, that we may know who is responsible for this evil that is on us.” So they cast lots, and the lot fell on Jonah. <sup>8</sup>Then they asked him, “Tell us, please, for whose cause this evil is on us. What is your occupation? Where do you come from? What is your country? Of what people are you?”

<sup>9</sup>He said to them, “I am a Hebrew, and I fear the LORD, the God[^1-4] of heaven, who has made the sea and the dry land.”

<sup>10</sup>Then the men were exceedingly afraid, and said to him, “What have you done?” For the men knew that he was fleeing from the presence of the LORD, because he had told them. <sup>11</sup>Then they said to him, “What shall we do to you, that the sea may be calm to us?” For the sea grew more and more stormy.

<sup>12</sup>He said to them, “Take me up, and throw me into the sea. Then the sea will be calm for you; for I know that because of me this great storm is on you.”

<sup>13</sup>Nevertheless the men rowed hard to get them back to the land; but they could not, for the sea grew more and more stormy against them. <sup>14</sup>Therefore they cried to the LORD, and said, “We beg you, LORD, we beg you, don’t let us die for this man’s life, and don’t lay on us innocent blood; for you, LORD, have done as it pleased you.” <sup>15</sup>So they took up Jonah and threw him into the sea; and the sea ceased its raging. <sup>16</sup>Then the men feared the LORD exceedingly; and they offered a sacrifice to the LORD and made vows.

<sup>17</sup>The LORD prepared a huge fish to swallow up Jonah, and Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights.

[^1-1]: When rendered in ALL CAPITAL LETTERS, “LORD” or “GOD” is the translation of God’s Proper Name (Hebrew “יהוה”, usually pronounced Yahweh).
[^1-2]: or, gods
[^1-3]: or, gods
[^1-4]: The Hebrew word rendered “God” is “אֱלֹהִ֑ים” (Elohim).

## Jonah 2

> **Date of events:** c. 780–753 BC (setting under Jeroboam II)
>
> Jonah's prayer "out of the fish's belly" (2:1). The psalm of thanksgiving (2:2–9) is made up largely of phrases found in the Psalms, and many scholars think it was added to the story; others see it as integral.
>
> **Basis:**
> - [Jeroboam II, Uzziah and the Assyrian advance](../reference/anchors.md#eighth-century), c. 793–722 BC. Jeroboam II (793–753) and Uzziah (792–740) ruled in prosperity; Tiglath-pileser III (745–727) then pushed west, Israel and Aram attacked Judah (the Syro-Ephraimite war, 734–732) and Damascus fell in 732. The eclipse of 763 BC in the Assyrian eponym list fixes these dates.
>
> **Sources:** [Sasson 1990](../reference/sources.md#sasson-jonah); [Assyrian eponym list](../reference/sources.md#eponym-canon); [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Cogan; Cogan & Tadmor](../reference/sources.md#cogan-kings); [ANET](../reference/sources.md#anet).
>
> **Book written:** 8th century BC (traditional) or c. 500–300 BC (most critical scholars); see [the date of Jonah](#date-of-writing).

<sup>1</sup>Then Jonah prayed to the LORD, his God, out of the fish’s belly. <sup>2</sup>He said,

“I called because of my affliction to the LORD.  
&emsp;&emsp;He answered me.  
Out of the belly of Sheol[^2-1] I cried.  
&emsp;&emsp;You heard my voice.  
<sup>3</sup>For you threw me into the depths,  
&emsp;&emsp;in the heart of the seas.  
The flood was all around me.  
&emsp;&emsp;All your waves and your billows passed over me.  
<sup>4</sup>I said, ‘I have been banished from your sight;  
&emsp;&emsp;yet I will look again toward your holy temple.’  
<sup>5</sup>The waters surrounded me,  
&emsp;&emsp;even to the soul.  
The deep was around me.  
&emsp;&emsp;The weeds were wrapped around my head.  
<sup>6</sup>I went down to the bottoms of the mountains.  
&emsp;&emsp;The earth barred me in forever;  
&emsp;&emsp;yet you have brought my life up from the pit, LORD my God.  
<sup>7</sup>“When my soul fainted within me, I remembered the LORD.  
&emsp;&emsp;My prayer came in to you, into your holy temple.  
<sup>8</sup>Those who regard vain idols forsake their own mercy.  
&emsp;&emsp;<sup>9</sup>But I will sacrifice to you with the voice of thanksgiving.  
&emsp;&emsp;I will pay that which I have vowed.  
Salvation belongs to the LORD.”  

<sup>10</sup>Then the LORD spoke to the fish, and it vomited out Jonah on the dry land.

[^2-1]: Sheol is the place of the dead.

## Jonah 3

> **Date of events:** c. 780–753 BC (setting under Jeroboam II)
>
> Nineveh repents at Jonah's preaching. Assyrian kings lived at Calah (Nimrud) in the 8th century; Nineveh became the capital under Sennacherib (c. 700 BC). Some conservative scholars link the city's openness to the troubles of Ashur-dan III (773–756 BC), with revolts, plague and the eclipse of 763 BC recorded in the eponym list. Critical scholars point to "Nineveh was an exceedingly great city" (3:3) and the "king of Nineveh" (3:6) as signs of later writing.
>
> **Basis:**
> - [Jeroboam II, Uzziah and the Assyrian advance](../reference/anchors.md#eighth-century), c. 793–722 BC. Jeroboam II (793–753) and Uzziah (792–740) ruled in prosperity; Tiglath-pileser III (745–727) then pushed west, Israel and Aram attacked Judah (the Syro-Ephraimite war, 734–732) and Damascus fell in 732. The eclipse of 763 BC in the Assyrian eponym list fixes these dates.
>
> **Sources:** [Assyrian eponym list](../reference/sources.md#eponym-canon); [Sasson 1990](../reference/sources.md#sasson-jonah); [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Cogan; Cogan & Tadmor](../reference/sources.md#cogan-kings); [ANET](../reference/sources.md#anet).
>
> **Book written:** 8th century BC (traditional) or c. 500–300 BC (most critical scholars); see [the date of Jonah](#date-of-writing).

<sup>1</sup>The LORD’s word came to Jonah the second time, saying, <sup>2</sup>“Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and preach to it the message that I give you.”

<sup>3</sup>So Jonah arose, and went to Nineveh, according to the LORD’s word. Now Nineveh was an exceedingly great city, three days’ journey across. <sup>4</sup>Jonah began to enter into the city a day’s journey, and he cried out, and said, “In forty days, Nineveh will be overthrown!”

<sup>5</sup>The people of Nineveh believed God; and they proclaimed a fast and put on sackcloth, from their greatest even to their least. <sup>6</sup>The news reached the king of Nineveh, and he arose from his throne, took off his royal robe, covered himself with sackcloth, and sat in ashes. <sup>7</sup>He made a proclamation and published through Nineveh by the decree of the king and his nobles, saying, “Let neither man nor animal, herd nor flock, taste anything; let them not feed, nor drink water; <sup>8</sup>but let them be covered with sackcloth, both man and animal, and let them cry mightily to God. Yes, let them turn everyone from his evil way and from the violence that is in his hands. <sup>9</sup>Who knows whether God will not turn and relent, and turn away from his fierce anger, so that we might not perish?”

<sup>10</sup>God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way. God relented of the disaster which he said he would do to them, and he didn’t do it.

## Jonah 4

> **Date of events:** c. 780–753 BC (setting under Jeroboam II)
>
> Jonah's anger at God's mercy and the lesson of the plant. Nineveh has "more than one hundred twenty thousand persons who can't discern between their right hand and their left hand" (4:11). No new time indication; the same setting as chapters 1–3.
>
> **Basis:**
> - [Jeroboam II, Uzziah and the Assyrian advance](../reference/anchors.md#eighth-century), c. 793–722 BC. Jeroboam II (793–753) and Uzziah (792–740) ruled in prosperity; Tiglath-pileser III (745–727) then pushed west, Israel and Aram attacked Judah (the Syro-Ephraimite war, 734–732) and Damascus fell in 732. The eclipse of 763 BC in the Assyrian eponym list fixes these dates.
>
> **Sources:** [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Assyrian eponym list](../reference/sources.md#eponym-canon); [Cogan; Cogan & Tadmor](../reference/sources.md#cogan-kings); [ANET](../reference/sources.md#anet).
>
> **Book written:** 8th century BC (traditional) or c. 500–300 BC (most critical scholars); see [the date of Jonah](#date-of-writing).

<sup>1</sup>But it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was angry. <sup>2</sup>He prayed to the LORD, and said, “Please, LORD, wasn’t this what I said when I was still in my own country? Therefore I hurried to flee to Tarshish, for I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness, and you relent of doing harm. <sup>3</sup>Therefore now, LORD, take, I beg you, my life from me, for it is better for me to die than to live.”

<sup>4</sup>The LORD said, “Is it right for you to be angry?”

<sup>5</sup>Then Jonah went out of the city and sat on the east side of the city, and there made himself a booth and sat under it in the shade, until he might see what would become of the city. <sup>6</sup>The LORD God prepared a vine and made it to come up over Jonah, that it might be a shade over his head to deliver him from his discomfort. So Jonah was exceedingly glad because of the vine. <sup>7</sup>But God prepared a worm at dawn the next day, and it chewed on the vine so that it withered. <sup>8</sup>When the sun arose, God prepared a sultry east wind; and the sun beat on Jonah’s head, so that he was faint and requested for himself that he might die. He said, “It is better for me to die than to live.”

<sup>9</sup>God said to Jonah, “Is it right for you to be angry about the vine?”

He said, “I am right to be angry, even to death.”

<sup>10</sup>The LORD said, “You have been concerned for the vine, for which you have not labored, neither made it grow; which came up in a night and perished in a night. <sup>11</sup>Shouldn’t I be concerned for Nineveh, that great city, in which are more than one hundred twenty thousand persons who can’t discern between their right hand and their left hand, and also many animals?”
