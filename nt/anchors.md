# Chronological anchors

The fixed points the chapter dates depend on. Each chapter heading quotes
the short form; this page gives the reasoning and the alternatives.
Years: negative ranges are BC; there is no year 0.

<a id="birth"></a>

## Birth of Jesus: c. 6–4 BC

Before Herod the Great's death (Matt 2:1, 19; Luke 1:5), which most historians put in spring 4 BC; a minority argue for 1 BC, which puts the birth c. 3–2 BC.

**Herod's death.** Matthew and Luke both place the birth in Herod the Great's
reign. Josephus dates Herod's death shortly after a lunar eclipse and before a
Passover (*Ant.* 17.167, 17.213) and gives reign lengths (*Ant.* 17.191) that,
with the reigns of his sons, point to 4 BC. That is the standard view
(Schürer; Finegan; Brown; Meier; Sanders). Matthew 2:16 ("two years old and
under") suggests the birth came a year or two before Herod died, hence
"c. 6–4 BC".

**Minority: 1 BC.** Filmer (1966) and Steinmann (2009) argue that Herod died in
early 1 BC (associated with the total lunar eclipse of January 1 BC), which
allows a birth c. 3–2 BC. That date fits the early-church tradition (Irenaeus,
Clement of Alexandria and others put the birth around 3–2 BC). Finegan's
handbook sets out both reckonings.

**Quirinius's census (Luke 2:2).** Josephus dates Quirinius's census of Judea
to AD 6, when Archelaus was deposed (*Ant.* 17.355; 18.1–2, 18.26). Brown treats
Luke's dating as a historical confusion; others propose an earlier registration
or translate *prōtē* as "before" the governorship of Quirinius. That census is
the main tension in any birth date and remains unresolved.

**Sources:** [Josephus, Antiquities, 17.167, 17.191, 17.213](sources.md#josephus-ant); [Schürer (rev.) 1973](sources.md#schurer); [Finegan 1998](sources.md#finegan); [Brown, Birth of the Messiah, Appendix VII](sources.md#brown-birth); [Meier 1991, ch. 11](sources.md#meier); [Filmer 1966](sources.md#filmer); [Steinmann 2009](sources.md#steinmann-herod).

<a id="childhood"></a>

## Jesus at twelve in the temple: c. AD 6–9

Luke 2:42 says Jesus was twelve; counting from a birth c. 6–4 BC that is c. AD 7–9 (c. AD 9–10 from a 3–2 BC birth).

No external synchronism; the date simply follows from the birth date plus
twelve years. It falls near the deposition of Archelaus and Quirinius's census
(AD 6).

**Sources:** [Finegan 1998](sources.md#finegan); [Brown, Birth of the Messiah](sources.md#brown-birth).

<a id="models"></a>

## Two models for the ministry of Jesus: AD 27/28–30 or AD 29–33

Model A: ministry c. AD 27/28–30, crucifixion 7 April AD 30. Model B: ministry AD 29–33, crucifixion 3 April AD 33. Both are held widely, so chapters set in the ministry give a date for each model.

Every date inside Jesus' ministry depends on two disputed points: when it began
(Luke 3:1) and the year of the crucifixion (AD 30 or 33). John names three
Passovers (2:13; 6:4; 11:55 and following), so on John's outline the ministry
lasted at least two years and some months. The Synoptics, read alone, could fit
a single year.

| | Model A | Model B |
|---|---|---|
| Baptism and first disciples | late AD 27 / early 28 | late AD 28 / 29 |
| First Passover (John 2:13) | AD 28 | AD 30 |
| Feeding of the 5,000, Passover (John 6:4) | AD 29 | AD 32 |
| Tabernacles, Dedication (John 7–10) | autumn–winter AD 29 | autumn–winter AD 32 |
| Crucifixion | Friday 7 April AD 30 | Friday 3 April AD 33 |
| Held by (examples) | Meier; Riesner; Sanders (c. 30) | Hoehner; Humphreys & Waddington; Steinmann |

Model A has to read Luke's "fifteenth year of Tiberius" from a co-regency
(AD 11/12) or by a Syrian/Jewish regnal-year count (making AD 27/28 the
fifteenth year), or else shorten the ministry. Model B takes Luke 3:1 at face
value (AD 28/29), but needs a ministry of about 3½–4 years, with an extra
Passover implied between John 2 and John 6 (often seen in John 5:1).

Dates inside the ministry are approximate under either model: the Gospels
arrange much of their material by topic, not in strict order.

**Sources:** [Meier 1991, ch. 11](sources.md#meier); [Hoehner 1977](sources.md#hoehner); [Finegan 1998](sources.md#finegan); [Brown, Death of the Messiah](sources.md#brown-death); [Humphreys & Waddington 1983](sources.md#humphreys-waddington).

<a id="ministry-start"></a>

## Start of John the Baptist's preaching and Jesus' baptism: AD 27–29

Luke 3:1 dates John's preaching to the 15th year of Tiberius: AD 28/29 counted from Augustus' death (19 Aug AD 14), or AD 26/27 or 27/28 on other reckonings. John 2:20 ("46 years" building the temple) gives AD 27/28 or 29/30, depending on what Herod's building start refers to.

**Luke 3:1–2.** Tiberius succeeded Augustus on 19 August AD 14. Counting his
years from then, the 15th year is AD 28/29 (Hoehner; most historians). Some
count from his co-regency with Augustus (AD 11/12), which gives AD 26/27, or
use the Syrian/Jewish autumn new year with an accession-year count, which gives
autumn AD 27–28 (Finegan sets out the options). Pilate (AD 26–36/37), Antipas
(4 BC–AD 39), Philip (4 BC–AD 33/34) and the high priest Caiaphas (c. AD 18–36)
all fit.

**Luke 3:23.** Jesus was "about thirty": loose, but consistent with a birth
c. 6–4 BC and a start in AD 27–29.

**John 2:20.** "Forty-six years this temple has been in building." Josephus
dates the start of Herod's rebuilding to his 18th year, 20/19 BC (*Ant.*
15.380). Forty-six years on is AD 27/28 (Model A). Hoehner reads *naos* as the
sanctuary building, finished c. 18/17 BC, which puts the remark at Passover
AD 30 (Model B).

**Sources:** [Hoehner 1977](sources.md#hoehner); [Finegan 1998](sources.md#finegan); [Meier 1991, ch. 11](sources.md#meier); [Josephus, Antiquities, 15.380](sources.md#josephus-ant).

<a id="john-baptist-death"></a>

## Death of John the Baptist: c. AD 28–32

John was beheaded by Herod Antipas at Machaerus (Josephus, *Ant.* 18.116–119) during Jesus' Galilean ministry, before the Passover of John 6:4: c. AD 28–29 (Model A) or c. AD 31–32 (Model B).

Josephus confirms the execution and links it with Antipas's marriage to
Herodias and his later defeat by Aretas IV (AD 36), which people saw as
punishment. The Gospels place it before the feeding of the 5,000, which John
6:4 sets near a Passover.

**Sources:** [Josephus, Antiquities, 18.116–119](sources.md#josephus-ant); [Hoehner 1977](sources.md#hoehner); [Meier 1991, ch. 11](sources.md#meier).

<a id="passion-week"></a>

## Last week in Jerusalem: AD 30 or 33 (Passover week)

The final Passover week: Sunday 2 – Friday 7 April AD 30 (Model A) or Sunday 29 March – Friday 3 April AD 33 (Model B).

The Synoptics present the Last Supper as a Passover meal (Mark 14:12), so the
crucifixion falls on Nisan 15. John puts the crucifixion on the Day of
Preparation, while the Passover lambs were being slaughtered (John 18:28;
19:14), so Nisan 14. Astronomical reconstructions find Nisan 14 on a Friday in
AD 30 and AD 33, but Nisan 15 on a Friday in neither year (unless the calendar
was off by a day), which favours John's timing (Humphreys & Waddington 1983).
Humphreys (2011) proposes a Wednesday Last Supper by an older calendar to
reconcile the two accounts. The weekday dates given here follow John's
Nisan 14 Friday.

**Sources:** [Humphreys & Waddington 1983](sources.md#humphreys-waddington); [Humphreys 2011](sources.md#humphreys-2011); [Brown, Death of the Messiah](sources.md#brown-death); [Finegan 1998](sources.md#finegan).

<a id="crucifixion"></a>

## Crucifixion: 7 April AD 30 or 3 April AD 33

Under Pontius Pilate (AD 26–36/37; Tacitus, *Ann.* 15.44). A Friday on Nisan 14 only fits AD 30 or AD 33. AD 30 (Meier, Riesner) and AD 33 (Hoehner, Humphreys & Waddington) are the two main views.

**Fixed points.** Pilate's prefecture (AD 26–36/37), Caiaphas's high
priesthood (c. AD 18–36), and a Friday at Passover. Within those limits, a
Friday Nisan 14 falls only in AD 30 (7 April) and AD 33 (3 April) on
astronomical reconstruction (Humphreys & Waddington 1983).

**AD 30**: fits Luke 3:1 counted early and a 2–3 year ministry; Meier, Riesner
and many others. Brown (*Death of the Messiah*) narrows it to 30 or 33.

**AD 33**: fits Luke 3:1 counted from AD 14 with a 3½-year ministry. Hoehner
also points to Pilate's conduct after Sejanus fell (AD 31), and Humphreys &
Waddington to a partial lunar eclipse on 3 April AD 33 (cf. Acts 2:20).
Steinmann also holds 33.

**Others.** Kokkinos argues for AD 36, tied to Antipas's defeat. Bond (2013)
argues the evidence only allows c. AD 29–34. Sanders gives "c. 30".

**Sources:** [Tacitus, Annals, 15.44](sources.md#tacitus-ann); [Humphreys & Waddington 1983](sources.md#humphreys-waddington); [Meier 1991, ch. 11](sources.md#meier); [Brown, Death of the Messiah](sources.md#brown-death); [Hoehner 1977](sources.md#hoehner); [Riesner 1998](sources.md#riesner); [Kokkinos 1989](sources.md#kokkinos); [Bond 2013](sources.md#bond); [Sanders 1993](sources.md#sanders).

<a id="resurrection-ascension"></a>

## Resurrection appearances and ascension: April–May AD 30 or 33

The resurrection is the Sunday after the crucifixion (9 April AD 30 or 5 April AD 33); Acts 1:3 puts the ascension 40 days later (mid-May) and Pentecost 10 days after that (Acts 2:1).

These dates follow directly from the crucifixion date.

**Sources:** [Finegan 1998](sources.md#finegan); [Hoehner 1977](sources.md#hoehner).

<a id="early-church"></a>

## The early Jerusalem church to Stephen: c. AD 30–34

Acts 2–7 covers the period between Pentecost and Stephen's death. Stephen's death came shortly before Paul's conversion (Acts 8:1; 9:1), so it is usually dated c. AD 31–34.

No external synchronisms. The upper limit is Paul's conversion (see that
anchor); some put Stephen's stoning in the interval after Pilate was recalled
(AD 36/37), when the Sanhedrin might have acted without a governor, but most
date it earlier.

**Sources:** [Riesner 1998](sources.md#riesner); [Bruce 1977](sources.md#bruce-paul); [Finegan 1998](sources.md#finegan).

<a id="paul-conversion"></a>

## Conversion of Paul: c. AD 31–36 (often c. 33–34)

Galatians 1:18 and 2:1 count 3 + 14 (or 14 inclusive) years back from the Jerusalem meeting of c. AD 48–49, which gives c. AD 31–36. Riesner proposes AD 31/32, Jewett AD 34, Bruce c. 33.

**Galatians.** "After three years" Paul went up to Jerusalem (Gal 1:18);
"after fourteen years" he went up again (Gal 2:1). Whether the 14 years count
from the conversion or from the first visit, and whether years are counted
inclusively, moves the conversion by up to 3 years.

**Aretas (2 Cor 11:32).** Paul's escape from Damascus "under King Aretas" has to
fall before Aretas IV's death (c. AD 40). Some argue that Nabataean control of
Damascus is likelier after AD 37. Jewett's chronology uses this point heavily
(conversion AD 34, escape AD 37).

**Views.** Riesner AD 31/32; Bruce c. 33; Jewett 34; Lüdemann 30 or 33 (his
chronology puts much of the Greek mission before the Jerusalem council).

**Sources:** [Riesner 1998](sources.md#riesner); [Jewett 1979](sources.md#jewett); [Bruce 1977](sources.md#bruce-paul); [Lüdemann 1984](sources.md#luedemann).

<a id="agrippa-death"></a>

## Death of Herod Agrippa I: AD 44

Josephus describes Agrippa I's sudden death at Caesarea in AD 44 (*Ant.* 19.343–352), the same event as Acts 12:20–23, so it is one of the firmest dates in Acts.

Agrippa ruled Judea AD 41–44. Josephus places his death after he had reigned
three years over all Judea, at games in Caesarea. James son of Zebedee was
executed shortly before (Acts 12:1–2), probably around Passover AD 43 or 44.

**Sources:** [Josephus, Antiquities, 19.343–352](sources.md#josephus-ant); [Schürer (rev.) 1973](sources.md#schurer); [Riesner 1998](sources.md#riesner).

<a id="famine"></a>

## Famine under Claudius: c. AD 45–48

Acts 11:28 places Agabus's famine "in the days of Claudius" (AD 41–54). Josephus reports a severe famine in Judea under the procurators Fadus and Tiberius Alexander, c. AD 45–48 (*Ant.* 20.101).

Luke puts the prophecy before Agrippa's death (Acts 12), but the famine-relief
visit of Barnabas and Saul (Acts 11:30; 12:25) probably took place c. AD 45–47.
Some identify it with Galatians 2:1–10.

**Sources:** [Josephus, Antiquities, 20.51–53, 20.101](sources.md#josephus-ant); [Riesner 1998](sources.md#riesner).

<a id="council"></a>

## Jerusalem council: c. AD 48–49

Acts 15 comes after the first journey and before Paul reached Corinth (c. AD 50). Most date it c. AD 48–49. Galatians 2:1 ("after fourteen years") fits that date.

Paul arrived in Corinth after Aquila and Priscilla had come from Rome under
Claudius's edict (Acts 18:2; AD 49 per Orosius) and was there when Gallio
arrived (AD 51), so the council has to come before c. AD 50. Whether Galatians
2:1–10 describes the council visit (Acts 15) or the famine visit (Acts 11:30)
is one of the main disputes in Pauline chronology. Lüdemann dates the council
later (c. AD 50/51) and puts Paul's first visit to Greece before it.

**Sources:** [Riesner 1998](sources.md#riesner); [Jewett 1979](sources.md#jewett); [Bruce 1977](sources.md#bruce-paul); [Lüdemann 1984](sources.md#luedemann).

<a id="claudius-edict"></a>

## Claudius expels Jews from Rome: AD 49 (minority AD 41)

Suetonius (*Claudius* 25.4) reports the expulsion of Jews "agitating at the instigation of Chrestus". Orosius (7.6.15) dates it to Claudius's 9th year, AD 49. Aquila and Priscilla had "recently" arrived from Rome (Acts 18:2).

Most follow Orosius's AD 49. Lüdemann identifies the edict with the measure
Dio describes for AD 41 (Dio 60.6.6: Jews not expelled but forbidden to
assemble), which supports his early dating of Paul's mission in Greece.

**Sources:** [Suetonius, Claudius, 25.4](sources.md#suetonius-claud); [Orosius, History, 7.6.15](sources.md#orosius); [Cassius Dio, Roman History, 60.6.6](sources.md#dio); [Riesner 1998](sources.md#riesner); [Lüdemann 1984](sources.md#luedemann).

<a id="gallio"></a>

## Gallio proconsul of Achaia: AD 51–52

The Delphi inscription dates Gallio's proconsulship to c. July AD 51 – June 52 (some say 50–51). Paul appeared before him (Acts 18:12) after about 18 months in Corinth (18:11), which puts Paul there c. AD 50–52: the firmest point in Paul's chronology.

Claudius's letter to Delphi names Gallio as proconsul and is dated by Claudius's
26th acclamation (first half of AD 52). Proconsuls served for a year from early
summer, so Gallio most likely held office 51–52 (Murphy-O'Connor; Riesner), and
possibly 50–51. Paul's case most likely came early in Gallio's term (summer or
autumn 51), so Paul came to Corinth c. early 50 and left c. 51/52.

**Sources:** [Delphi (Gallio) inscription](sources.md#gallio-inscription); [Murphy-O'Connor 2002](sources.md#murphy-oconnor); [Riesner 1998](sources.md#riesner); [Jewett 1979](sources.md#jewett).

<a id="ephesus"></a>

## Paul's Ephesian ministry: c. AD 52/53–55/56

After Corinth and a trip to Jerusalem and Antioch (Acts 18:22), Paul spent "three years" in Ephesus (Acts 19:8–10; 20:31): c. AD 52/53–55/56.

It follows from the Gallio date and Luke's durations. 1 Corinthians was written
from Ephesus in this period (1 Cor 16:8).

**Sources:** [Riesner 1998](sources.md#riesner); [Jewett 1979](sources.md#jewett); [Bruce 1977](sources.md#bruce-paul).

<a id="paul-arrest"></a>

## Paul's last journey to Jerusalem and arrest: AD 57 (some say 56 or 58)

After winter in Corinth (Acts 20:2–3) Paul left Philippi after Passover (20:6) to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost (20:16). Riesner, Jewett and most others put this in AD 57; others say 56 or 58.

The year is counted back from Festus's arrival (Felix kept Paul two years,
Acts 24:27) and forward from the Ephesian ministry. Romans, written from
Corinth just before the trip (Rom 15:25), is dated accordingly (AD 56–57).

**Sources:** [Riesner 1998](sources.md#riesner); [Jewett 1979](sources.md#jewett); [Bruce 1977](sources.md#bruce-paul).

<a id="festus"></a>

## Felix replaced by Festus: AD 59 or 60 (minority AD 55/56)

Felix (procurator c. AD 52–59/60) kept Paul two years (Acts 24:27) before Porcius Festus succeeded him. Most date the change to AD 59 or 60 (Schürer, Riesner); a minority argue for AD 55/56.

Josephus (*Ant.* 20.182) says Felix was accused before Nero after his recall
and saved by his brother Pallas. Some put the change early (AD 55/56) because
Pallas lost influence in AD 55; most argue that Josephus's narrative and
provincial coinage favour AD 59 or 60. Festus died in office and was succeeded
by Albinus in AD 62 (after the stoning of James).

**Sources:** [Josephus, Antiquities, 20.182](sources.md#josephus-ant); [Schürer (rev.) 1973](sources.md#schurer); [Riesner 1998](sources.md#riesner); [Jewett 1979](sources.md#jewett).

<a id="paul-rome"></a>

## Voyage to Rome and Roman custody: AD 59/60–62 (or 60/61–63)

Paul sailed after "the Fast" (the Day of Atonement, Acts 27:9) in autumn, wintered on Malta, and reached Rome in spring (Acts 28:11–16): AD 60 on the common Festus date. He then spent two years under house arrest (Acts 28:30), to c. AD 62.

Follows from the Festus date. Ramsay argued for autumn AD 59, partly because
the Day of Atonement fell late that year, which makes Luke's remark about
dangerous sailing apt. Acts ends without Paul's trial, which some take as a
sign it was written c. AD 62 (Hemer; Robinson).

**Sources:** [Ramsay 1895](sources.md#ramsay); [Riesner 1998](sources.md#riesner); [Hemer 1989](sources.md#hemer); [Robinson 1976](sources.md#robinson).

<a id="nero"></a>

## Nero's persecution and the deaths of Peter and Paul: AD 64–67/68

Tacitus (*Ann.* 15.44) describes Nero blaming Christians for the fire of Rome (July AD 64). Early tradition puts Peter's and Paul's martyrdoms in Rome under Nero (1 Clement 5; Eusebius, *H.E.* 2.25), c. AD 64–67.

Letters attributed to Peter and Paul's last letters (2 Timothy), if authentic,
date before these deaths. Eusebius's *Chronicle* tradition puts Paul's death in
AD 67/68; many modern historians link both martyrdoms to AD 64–65.

**Sources:** [Tacitus, Annals, 15.44](sources.md#tacitus-ann); [1 Clement, 5](sources.md#clement-1); [Eusebius, Church History, 2.22, 2.25](sources.md#eusebius-he).

<a id="james-death"></a>

## Death of James, brother of Jesus: AD 62

Josephus (*Ant.* 20.197–203) reports that the high priest Ananus had James stoned between Festus's death and Albinus's arrival, AD 62.

If the letter of James is by James of Jerusalem, it must be earlier than AD 62.

**Sources:** [Josephus, Antiquities, 20.197–203](sources.md#josephus-ant); [Schürer (rev.) 1973](sources.md#schurer).

<a id="temple-70"></a>

## Destruction of the Jerusalem temple: AD 70

Titus took Jerusalem and burned the temple in August–September AD 70 (Josephus, *War* 6). Whether a book seems to know of this event is the main argument for dating the Gospels, Acts and Hebrews.

**Post-70 dating.** Many scholars read Luke 19:43–44 and 21:20–24 and Matthew
22:7 as written after the siege, and date Matthew and Luke after 70 (Brown;
Ehrman). Mark 13 is often placed just before or just after 70.

**Pre-70 dating.** Others take the predictions as genuine prophecy or as too
general to require hindsight, and note that no NT book mentions the temple's
fall as past; Robinson (1976) dated the whole NT before 70.

**Sources:** [Josephus, Jewish War, 6.249–266](sources.md#josephus-war); [Brown 1997](sources.md#brown-intro); [Robinson 1976](sources.md#robinson); [Carson & Moo 2005](sources.md#carson-moo).

<a id="domitian"></a>

## Revelation and the reign of Domitian: c. AD 95 (minority AD 68–69)

Irenaeus says the Apocalypse was seen "towards the end of Domitian's reign" (AD 81–96), so c. AD 95; most modern scholars agree. A minority date it to AD 68–69, after Nero.

**Late (c. 95).** Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.30.3 (quoted by Eusebius,
*H.E.* 3.18, 5.8); the use of "Babylon" for Rome, a name that appears after
AD 70; the developed state of the churches in Rev 2–3; persecution in Asia.
Brown, Aune (final edition), Carson & Moo.

**Early (68–69).** The seven kings of Rev 17:9–11 counted from Augustus or
Julius; the temple apparently still standing in Rev 11:1–2; 666 as the
numerical value of "Nero Caesar" (Rev 13:18). Robinson; Gentry.

**Sources:** [Irenaeus, Against Heresies, 5.30.3](sources.md#irenaeus); [Eusebius, Church History, 3.18](sources.md#eusebius-he); [Brown 1997](sources.md#brown-intro); [Aune 1997](sources.md#aune); [Robinson 1976](sources.md#robinson); [Gentry 1989](sources.md#gentry).
