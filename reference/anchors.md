# Chronological anchors

The fixed points the chapter dates depend on. Each chapter heading quotes
the short form; this page gives the reasoning and the alternatives.
There is no year 0: 1 BC is followed by AD 1.

<a id="birth"></a>

## Birth of Jesus: c. 6–4 BC

Before Herod the Great's death (Matt 2:1, 19; Luke 1:5), which most historians put in spring 4 BC; a minority argue for 1 BC, which puts the birth c. 3–2 BC.

**Herod's death.** Matthew and Luke both place the birth in Herod the Great's
reign. Josephus dates Herod's death shortly after a lunar eclipse and before a
Passover (*Ant.* 17.167, 17.213) and gives reign lengths (*Ant.* 17.191) that,
with the reigns of his sons, point to 4 BC. That is the standard view
(Schürer; Finegan; Brown; Meier; Sanders). Matthew 2:16 ("two years old and
under") suggests the birth came a year or two before Herod died, hence
"c. 6–4 BC".

**Minority: 1 BC.** Filmer (1966) and Steinmann (2009) argue that Herod died in
early 1 BC (associated with the total lunar eclipse of January 1 BC), which
allows a birth c. 3–2 BC. That date fits the early-church tradition (Irenaeus,
Clement of Alexandria and others put the birth around 3–2 BC). Finegan's
handbook sets out both reckonings.

**Quirinius's census (Luke 2:2).** Josephus dates Quirinius's census of Judea
to AD 6, when Archelaus was deposed (*Ant.* 17.355; 18.1–2, 18.26). Brown treats
Luke's dating as a historical confusion; others propose an earlier registration
or translate *prōtē* as "before" the governorship of Quirinius. That census is
the main tension in any birth date and remains unresolved.

**Sources:** [Josephus, Antiquities, 17.167, 17.191, 17.213](../reference/sources.md#josephus-ant); [Schürer (rev.) 1973](../reference/sources.md#schurer); [Finegan 1998](../reference/sources.md#finegan); [Brown, Birth of the Messiah, Appendix VII](../reference/sources.md#brown-birth); [Meier 1991, ch. 11](../reference/sources.md#meier); [Filmer 1966](../reference/sources.md#filmer); [Steinmann 2009](../reference/sources.md#steinmann-herod).

<a id="childhood"></a>

## Jesus at twelve in the temple: c. AD 6–9

Luke 2:42 says Jesus was twelve; counting from a birth c. 6–4 BC that is c. AD 7–9 (c. AD 9–10 from a 3–2 BC birth).

No external synchronism; the date simply follows from the birth date plus
twelve years. It falls near the deposition of Archelaus and Quirinius's census
(AD 6).

**Sources:** [Finegan 1998](../reference/sources.md#finegan); [Brown, Birth of the Messiah](../reference/sources.md#brown-birth).

<a id="models"></a>

## Two models for the ministry of Jesus: AD 27/28–30 or AD 29–33

Model A: ministry c. AD 27/28–30, crucifixion 7 April AD 30. Model B: ministry AD 29–33, crucifixion 3 April AD 33. Both are held widely, so chapters set in the ministry give a date for each model.

Every date inside Jesus' ministry depends on two disputed points: when it began
(Luke 3:1) and the year of the crucifixion (AD 30 or 33). John names three
Passovers (2:13; 6:4; 11:55 and following), so on John's outline the ministry
lasted at least two years and some months. The Synoptics, read alone, could fit
a single year.

| | Model A | Model B |
|---|---|---|
| Baptism and first disciples | late AD 27 / early 28 | late AD 28 / 29 |
| First Passover (John 2:13) | AD 28 | AD 30 |
| Feeding of the 5,000, Passover (John 6:4) | AD 29 | AD 32 |
| Tabernacles, Dedication (John 7–10) | autumn–winter AD 29 | autumn–winter AD 32 |
| Crucifixion | Friday 7 April AD 30 | Friday 3 April AD 33 |
| Held by (examples) | Meier; Riesner; Sanders (c. 30) | Hoehner; Humphreys & Waddington; Steinmann |

Model A has to read Luke's "fifteenth year of Tiberius" from a co-regency
(AD 11/12) or by a Syrian/Jewish regnal-year count (making AD 27/28 the
fifteenth year), or else shorten the ministry. Model B takes Luke 3:1 at face
value (AD 28/29), but needs a ministry of about 3½–4 years, with an extra
Passover implied between John 2 and John 6 (often seen in John 5:1).

Dates inside the ministry are approximate under either model: the Gospels
arrange much of their material by topic, not in strict order.

**Sources:** [Meier 1991, ch. 11](../reference/sources.md#meier); [Hoehner 1977](../reference/sources.md#hoehner); [Finegan 1998](../reference/sources.md#finegan); [Brown, Death of the Messiah](../reference/sources.md#brown-death); [Humphreys & Waddington 1983](../reference/sources.md#humphreys-waddington).

<a id="ministry-start"></a>

## Start of John the Baptist's preaching and Jesus' baptism: AD 27–29

Luke 3:1 dates John's preaching to the 15th year of Tiberius: AD 28/29 counted from Augustus' death (19 Aug AD 14), or AD 26/27 or 27/28 on other reckonings. John 2:20 ("46 years" building the temple) gives AD 27/28 or 29/30, depending on what Herod's building start refers to.

**Luke 3:1–2.** Tiberius succeeded Augustus on 19 August AD 14. Counting his
years from then, the 15th year is AD 28/29 (Hoehner; most historians). Some
count from his co-regency with Augustus (AD 11/12), which gives AD 26/27, or
use the Syrian/Jewish autumn new year with an accession-year count, which gives
autumn AD 27–28 (Finegan sets out the options). Pilate (AD 26–36/37), Antipas
(4 BC–AD 39), Philip (4 BC–AD 33/34) and the high priest Caiaphas (c. AD 18–36)
all fit.

**Luke 3:23.** Jesus was "about thirty": loose, but consistent with a birth
c. 6–4 BC and a start in AD 27–29.

**John 2:20.** "Forty-six years this temple has been in building." Josephus
dates the start of Herod's rebuilding to his 18th year, 20/19 BC (*Ant.*
15.380). Forty-six years on is AD 27/28 (Model A). Hoehner reads *naos* as the
sanctuary building, finished c. 18/17 BC, which puts the remark at Passover
AD 30 (Model B).

**Sources:** [Hoehner 1977](../reference/sources.md#hoehner); [Finegan 1998](../reference/sources.md#finegan); [Meier 1991, ch. 11](../reference/sources.md#meier); [Josephus, Antiquities, 15.380](../reference/sources.md#josephus-ant).

<a id="john-baptist-death"></a>

## Death of John the Baptist: c. AD 28–32

John was beheaded by Herod Antipas at Machaerus (Josephus, *Ant.* 18.116–119) during Jesus' Galilean ministry, before the Passover of John 6:4: c. AD 28–29 (Model A) or c. AD 31–32 (Model B).

Josephus confirms the execution and links it with Antipas's marriage to
Herodias and his later defeat by Aretas IV (AD 36), which people saw as
punishment. The Gospels place it before the feeding of the 5,000, which John
6:4 sets near a Passover.

**Sources:** [Josephus, Antiquities, 18.116–119](../reference/sources.md#josephus-ant); [Hoehner 1977](../reference/sources.md#hoehner); [Meier 1991, ch. 11](../reference/sources.md#meier).

<a id="passion-week"></a>

## Last week in Jerusalem: AD 30 or 33 (Passover week)

The final Passover week: Sunday 2 – Friday 7 April AD 30 (Model A) or Sunday 29 March – Friday 3 April AD 33 (Model B).

The Synoptics present the Last Supper as a Passover meal (Mark 14:12), so the
crucifixion falls on Nisan 15. John puts the crucifixion on the Day of
Preparation, while the Passover lambs were being slaughtered (John 18:28;
19:14), so Nisan 14. Astronomical reconstructions find Nisan 14 on a Friday in
AD 30 and AD 33, but Nisan 15 on a Friday in neither year (unless the calendar
was off by a day), which favours John's timing (Humphreys & Waddington 1983).
Humphreys (2011) proposes a Wednesday Last Supper by an older calendar to
reconcile the two accounts. The weekday dates given here follow John's
Nisan 14 Friday.

**Sources:** [Humphreys & Waddington 1983](../reference/sources.md#humphreys-waddington); [Humphreys 2011](../reference/sources.md#humphreys-2011); [Brown, Death of the Messiah](../reference/sources.md#brown-death); [Finegan 1998](../reference/sources.md#finegan).

<a id="crucifixion"></a>

## Crucifixion: 7 April AD 30 or 3 April AD 33

Under Pontius Pilate (AD 26–36/37; Tacitus, *Ann.* 15.44). A Friday on Nisan 14 only fits AD 30 or AD 33. AD 30 (Meier, Riesner) and AD 33 (Hoehner, Humphreys & Waddington) are the two main views.

**Fixed points.** Pilate's prefecture (AD 26–36/37), Caiaphas's high
priesthood (c. AD 18–36), and a Friday at Passover. Within those limits, a
Friday Nisan 14 falls only in AD 30 (7 April) and AD 33 (3 April) on
astronomical reconstruction (Humphreys & Waddington 1983).

**AD 30**: fits Luke 3:1 counted early and a 2–3 year ministry; Meier, Riesner
and many others. Brown (*Death of the Messiah*) narrows it to 30 or 33.

**AD 33**: fits Luke 3:1 counted from AD 14 with a 3½-year ministry. Hoehner
also points to Pilate's conduct after Sejanus fell (AD 31), and Humphreys &
Waddington to a partial lunar eclipse on 3 April AD 33 (cf. Acts 2:20).
Steinmann also holds 33.

**Others.** Kokkinos argues for AD 36, tied to Antipas's defeat. Bond (2013)
argues the evidence only allows c. AD 29–34. Sanders gives "c. 30".

**Sources:** [Tacitus, Annals, 15.44](../reference/sources.md#tacitus-ann); [Humphreys & Waddington 1983](../reference/sources.md#humphreys-waddington); [Meier 1991, ch. 11](../reference/sources.md#meier); [Brown, Death of the Messiah](../reference/sources.md#brown-death); [Hoehner 1977](../reference/sources.md#hoehner); [Riesner 1998](../reference/sources.md#riesner); [Kokkinos 1989](../reference/sources.md#kokkinos); [Bond 2013](../reference/sources.md#bond); [Sanders 1993](../reference/sources.md#sanders).

<a id="resurrection-ascension"></a>

## Resurrection appearances and ascension: April–May AD 30 or 33

The resurrection is the Sunday after the crucifixion (9 April AD 30 or 5 April AD 33); Acts 1:3 puts the ascension 40 days later (mid-May) and Pentecost 10 days after that (Acts 2:1).

These dates follow directly from the crucifixion date.

**Sources:** [Finegan 1998](../reference/sources.md#finegan); [Hoehner 1977](../reference/sources.md#hoehner).

<a id="early-church"></a>

## The early Jerusalem church to Stephen: c. AD 30–34

Acts 2–7 covers the period between Pentecost and Stephen's death. Stephen's death came shortly before Paul's conversion (Acts 8:1; 9:1), so it is usually dated c. AD 31–34.

No external synchronisms. The upper limit is Paul's conversion (see that
anchor); some put Stephen's stoning in the interval after Pilate was recalled
(AD 36/37), when the Sanhedrin might have acted without a governor, but most
date it earlier.

**Sources:** [Riesner 1998](../reference/sources.md#riesner); [Bruce 1977](../reference/sources.md#bruce-paul); [Finegan 1998](../reference/sources.md#finegan).

<a id="paul-conversion"></a>

## Conversion of Paul: c. AD 31–36 (often c. 33–34)

Galatians 1:18 and 2:1 count 3 + 14 (or 14 inclusive) years back from the Jerusalem meeting of c. AD 48–49, which gives c. AD 31–36. Riesner proposes AD 31/32, Jewett AD 34, Bruce c. 33.

**Galatians.** "After three years" Paul went up to Jerusalem (Gal 1:18);
"after fourteen years" he went up again (Gal 2:1). Whether the 14 years count
from the conversion or from the first visit, and whether years are counted
inclusively, moves the conversion by up to 3 years.

**Aretas (2 Cor 11:32).** Paul's escape from Damascus "under King Aretas" has to
fall before Aretas IV's death (c. AD 40). Some argue that Nabataean control of
Damascus is likelier after AD 37. Jewett's chronology uses this point heavily
(conversion AD 34, escape AD 37).

**Views.** Riesner AD 31/32; Bruce c. 33; Jewett 34; Lüdemann 30 or 33 (his
chronology puts much of the Greek mission before the Jerusalem council).

**Sources:** [Riesner 1998](../reference/sources.md#riesner); [Jewett 1979](../reference/sources.md#jewett); [Bruce 1977](../reference/sources.md#bruce-paul); [Lüdemann 1984](../reference/sources.md#luedemann).

<a id="agrippa-death"></a>

## Death of Herod Agrippa I: AD 44

Josephus describes Agrippa I's sudden death at Caesarea in AD 44 (*Ant.* 19.343–352), the same event as Acts 12:20–23, so it is one of the firmest dates in Acts.

Agrippa ruled Judea AD 41–44. Josephus places his death after he had reigned
three years over all Judea, at games in Caesarea. James son of Zebedee was
executed shortly before (Acts 12:1–2), probably around Passover AD 43 or 44.

**Sources:** [Josephus, Antiquities, 19.343–352](../reference/sources.md#josephus-ant); [Schürer (rev.) 1973](../reference/sources.md#schurer); [Riesner 1998](../reference/sources.md#riesner).

<a id="famine"></a>

## Famine under Claudius: c. AD 45–48

Acts 11:28 places Agabus's famine "in the days of Claudius" (AD 41–54). Josephus reports a severe famine in Judea under the procurators Fadus and Tiberius Alexander, c. AD 45–48 (*Ant.* 20.101).

Luke puts the prophecy before Agrippa's death (Acts 12), but the famine-relief
visit of Barnabas and Saul (Acts 11:30; 12:25) probably took place c. AD 45–47.
Some identify it with Galatians 2:1–10.

**Sources:** [Josephus, Antiquities, 20.51–53, 20.101](../reference/sources.md#josephus-ant); [Riesner 1998](../reference/sources.md#riesner).

<a id="council"></a>

## Jerusalem council: c. AD 48–49

Acts 15 comes after the first journey and before Paul reached Corinth (c. AD 50). Most date it c. AD 48–49. Galatians 2:1 ("after fourteen years") fits that date.

Paul arrived in Corinth after Aquila and Priscilla had come from Rome under
Claudius's edict (Acts 18:2; AD 49 per Orosius) and was there when Gallio
arrived (AD 51), so the council has to come before c. AD 50. Whether Galatians
2:1–10 describes the council visit (Acts 15) or the famine visit (Acts 11:30)
is one of the main disputes in Pauline chronology. Lüdemann dates the council
later (c. AD 50/51) and puts Paul's first visit to Greece before it.

**Sources:** [Riesner 1998](../reference/sources.md#riesner); [Jewett 1979](../reference/sources.md#jewett); [Bruce 1977](../reference/sources.md#bruce-paul); [Lüdemann 1984](../reference/sources.md#luedemann).

<a id="claudius-edict"></a>

## Claudius expels Jews from Rome: AD 49 (minority AD 41)

Suetonius (*Claudius* 25.4) reports the expulsion of Jews "agitating at the instigation of Chrestus". Orosius (7.6.15) dates it to Claudius's 9th year, AD 49. Aquila and Priscilla had "recently" arrived from Rome (Acts 18:2).

Most follow Orosius's AD 49. Lüdemann identifies the edict with the measure
Dio describes for AD 41 (Dio 60.6.6: Jews not expelled but forbidden to
assemble), which supports his early dating of Paul's mission in Greece.

**Sources:** [Suetonius, Claudius, 25.4](../reference/sources.md#suetonius-claud); [Orosius, History, 7.6.15](../reference/sources.md#orosius); [Cassius Dio, Roman History, 60.6.6](../reference/sources.md#dio); [Riesner 1998](../reference/sources.md#riesner); [Lüdemann 1984](../reference/sources.md#luedemann).

<a id="gallio"></a>

## Gallio proconsul of Achaia: AD 51–52

The Delphi inscription dates Gallio's proconsulship to c. July AD 51 – June 52 (some say 50–51). Paul appeared before him (Acts 18:12) after about 18 months in Corinth (18:11), which puts Paul there c. AD 50–52: the firmest point in Paul's chronology.

Claudius's letter to Delphi names Gallio as proconsul and is dated by Claudius's
26th acclamation (first half of AD 52). Proconsuls served for a year from early
summer, so Gallio most likely held office 51–52 (Murphy-O'Connor; Riesner), and
possibly 50–51. Paul's case most likely came early in Gallio's term (summer or
autumn 51), so Paul came to Corinth c. early 50 and left c. 51/52.

**Sources:** [Delphi (Gallio) inscription](../reference/sources.md#gallio-inscription); [Murphy-O'Connor 2002](../reference/sources.md#murphy-oconnor); [Riesner 1998](../reference/sources.md#riesner); [Jewett 1979](../reference/sources.md#jewett).

<a id="ephesus"></a>

## Paul's Ephesian ministry: c. AD 52/53–55/56

After Corinth and a trip to Jerusalem and Antioch (Acts 18:22), Paul spent "three years" in Ephesus (Acts 19:8–10; 20:31): c. AD 52/53–55/56.

It follows from the Gallio date and Luke's durations. 1 Corinthians was written
from Ephesus in this period (1 Cor 16:8).

**Sources:** [Riesner 1998](../reference/sources.md#riesner); [Jewett 1979](../reference/sources.md#jewett); [Bruce 1977](../reference/sources.md#bruce-paul).

<a id="paul-arrest"></a>

## Paul's last journey to Jerusalem and arrest: AD 57 (some say 56 or 58)

After winter in Corinth (Acts 20:2–3) Paul left Philippi after Passover (20:6) to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost (20:16). Riesner, Jewett and most others put this in AD 57; others say 56 or 58.

The year is counted back from Festus's arrival (Felix kept Paul two years,
Acts 24:27) and forward from the Ephesian ministry. Romans, written from
Corinth just before the trip (Rom 15:25), is dated accordingly (AD 56–57).

**Sources:** [Riesner 1998](../reference/sources.md#riesner); [Jewett 1979](../reference/sources.md#jewett); [Bruce 1977](../reference/sources.md#bruce-paul).

<a id="festus"></a>

## Felix replaced by Festus: AD 59 or 60 (minority AD 55/56)

Felix (procurator c. AD 52–59/60) kept Paul two years (Acts 24:27) before Porcius Festus succeeded him. Most date the change to AD 59 or 60 (Schürer, Riesner); a minority argue for AD 55/56.

Josephus (*Ant.* 20.182) says Felix was accused before Nero after his recall
and saved by his brother Pallas. Some put the change early (AD 55/56) because
Pallas lost influence in AD 55; most argue that Josephus's narrative and
provincial coinage favour AD 59 or 60. Festus died in office and was succeeded
by Albinus in AD 62 (after the stoning of James).

**Sources:** [Josephus, Antiquities, 20.182](../reference/sources.md#josephus-ant); [Schürer (rev.) 1973](../reference/sources.md#schurer); [Riesner 1998](../reference/sources.md#riesner); [Jewett 1979](../reference/sources.md#jewett).

<a id="paul-rome"></a>

## Voyage to Rome and Roman custody: AD 59/60–62 (or 60/61–63)

Paul sailed after "the Fast" (the Day of Atonement, Acts 27:9) in autumn, wintered on Malta, and reached Rome in spring (Acts 28:11–16): AD 60 on the common Festus date. He then spent two years under house arrest (Acts 28:30), to c. AD 62.

Follows from the Festus date. Ramsay argued for autumn AD 59, partly because
the Day of Atonement fell late that year, which makes Luke's remark about
dangerous sailing apt. Acts ends without Paul's trial, which some take as a
sign it was written c. AD 62 (Hemer; Robinson).

**Sources:** [Ramsay 1895](../reference/sources.md#ramsay); [Riesner 1998](../reference/sources.md#riesner); [Hemer 1989](../reference/sources.md#hemer); [Robinson 1976](../reference/sources.md#robinson).

<a id="nero"></a>

## Nero's persecution and the deaths of Peter and Paul: AD 64–67/68

Tacitus (*Ann.* 15.44) describes Nero blaming Christians for the fire of Rome (July AD 64). Early tradition puts Peter's and Paul's martyrdoms in Rome under Nero (1 Clement 5; Eusebius, *H.E.* 2.25), c. AD 64–67.

Letters attributed to Peter and Paul's last letters (2 Timothy), if authentic,
date before these deaths. Eusebius's *Chronicle* tradition puts Paul's death in
AD 67/68; many modern historians link both martyrdoms to AD 64–65.

**Sources:** [Tacitus, Annals, 15.44](../reference/sources.md#tacitus-ann); [1 Clement, 5](../reference/sources.md#clement-1); [Eusebius, Church History, 2.22, 2.25](../reference/sources.md#eusebius-he).

<a id="james-death"></a>

## Death of James, brother of Jesus: AD 62

Josephus (*Ant.* 20.197–203) reports that the high priest Ananus had James stoned between Festus's death and Albinus's arrival, AD 62.

If the letter of James is by James of Jerusalem, it must be earlier than AD 62.

**Sources:** [Josephus, Antiquities, 20.197–203](../reference/sources.md#josephus-ant); [Schürer (rev.) 1973](../reference/sources.md#schurer).

<a id="temple-70"></a>

## Destruction of the Jerusalem temple: AD 70

Titus took Jerusalem and burned the temple in August–September AD 70 (Josephus, *War* 6). Whether a book seems to know of this event is the main argument for dating the Gospels, Acts and Hebrews.

**Post-70 dating.** Many scholars read Luke 19:43–44 and 21:20–24 and Matthew
22:7 as written after the siege, and date Matthew and Luke after 70 (Brown;
Ehrman). Mark 13 is often placed just before or just after 70.

**Pre-70 dating.** Others take the predictions as genuine prophecy or as too
general to require hindsight, and note that no NT book mentions the temple's
fall as past; Robinson (1976) dated the whole NT before 70.

**Sources:** [Josephus, Jewish War, 6.249–266](../reference/sources.md#josephus-war); [Brown 1997](../reference/sources.md#brown-intro); [Robinson 1976](../reference/sources.md#robinson); [Carson & Moo 2005](../reference/sources.md#carson-moo).

<a id="domitian"></a>

## Revelation and the reign of Domitian: c. AD 95 (minority AD 68–69)

Irenaeus says the Apocalypse was seen "towards the end of Domitian's reign" (AD 81–96), so c. AD 95; most modern scholars agree. A minority date it to AD 68–69, after Nero.

**Late (c. 95).** Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.30.3 (quoted by Eusebius,
*H.E.* 3.18, 5.8); the use of "Babylon" for Rome, a name that appears after
AD 70; the developed state of the churches in Rev 2–3; persecution in Asia.
Brown, Aune (final edition), Carson & Moo.

**Early (68–69).** The seven kings of Rev 17:9–11 counted from Augustus or
Julius; the temple apparently still standing in Rev 11:1–2; 666 as the
numerical value of "Nero Caesar" (Rev 13:18). Robinson; Gentry.

**Sources:** [Irenaeus, Against Heresies, 5.30.3](../reference/sources.md#irenaeus); [Eusebius, Church History, 3.18](../reference/sources.md#eusebius-he); [Brown 1997](../reference/sources.md#brown-intro); [Aune 1997](../reference/sources.md#aune); [Robinson 1976](../reference/sources.md#robinson); [Gentry 1989](../reference/sources.md#gentry).

<a id="primeval"></a>

## Creation to Babel (Genesis 1–11): Undated (traditional reckonings 4004 BC and earlier)

No historical date can be given. Adding up the genealogies in the Hebrew text puts creation c. 4000 BC (Ussher 4004 BC, flood 2348 BC); the Greek Septuagint's larger numbers put it c. 5500 BC. Most scholars read these chapters as ancient Near Eastern origin stories, not datable history.

**Biblical arithmetic.** Genesis 5 and 11 give each ancestor's age at the birth
of a son. On the Masoretic (Hebrew) text this gives about 1,656 years from
creation to the flood and 2,000 years to Abraham. Ussher (1650) worked out
creation in 4004 BC and the flood in 2348 BC; the rabbinic *Seder Olam* gives
3761 BC, the start of the Jewish calendar era. The Septuagint and the Samaritan
Pentateuch have different numbers, so even the internal chronology depends on
which text is followed.

**Scholarly readings.** Most scholars, including many evangelicals, read
Genesis 1–11 as theological prologue in the forms of ancient Near Eastern
literature: Mesopotamian flood stories such as *Atrahasis* and the Gilgamesh
epic survive on tablets from the early 2nd millennium BC. Genealogies with gaps
are well known in the ancient world (Kitchen), so many conservative scholars
also decline to compute dates from them. The headings mark these chapters
*undated*.

**Sources:** [Ussher 1650](../reference/sources.md#ussher); [Seder Olam Rabbah](../reference/sources.md#seder-olam); [Lambert & Millard 1969](../reference/sources.md#lambert-millard); [Wenham 1987](../reference/sources.md#wenham-genesis); [Kitchen 2003](../reference/sources.md#kitchen); [Collins, Hebrew Bible](../reference/sources.md#collins-intro).

<a id="patriarchs"></a>

## Abraham, Isaac and Jacob: c. 2100–1700 BC (biblical figures and Kitchen) or undatable

Taking the Bible's numbers from the early exodus date, Abraham was born 2166 BC and Jacob went to Egypt in 1876 BC. Kitchen sets the stories in the Middle Bronze Age (c. 2000–1600 BC). Thompson and Van Seters argue that they were written in the 1st millennium BC and can't be dated as history.

**Biblical chronology.** 1 Kings 6:1 (480 years from the exodus to Solomon's
4th year, 967/966 BC) gives an exodus in 1446 BC; Exodus 12:40 (430 years in
Egypt) puts Jacob's arrival in 1876 BC; the ages in Genesis then put Abraham's
birth in 2166 BC. The Septuagint and Samaritan text of Exodus 12:40 include the
patriarchs' years in Canaan in the 430, which shortens the stay in Egypt to
about 215 years and moves the patriarchs about two centuries later. On the late
exodus date everything shifts down by about 190 years more.

**Middle Bronze Age setting.** Kitchen points to the price of slaves (20 shekels,
Gen 37:28), treaty forms and personal names typical of the early 2nd millennium.

**Late composition.** Thompson (1974) and Van Seters (1975) argued that the
parallels fit the 1st millennium as well or better, and that details such as
domesticated camels, Philistines and "Ur of the Chaldeans" belong to the Iron
Age, when the stories were written down. Many scholars now treat the patriarchs
as figures of tradition who can't be placed in a century.

**Sources:** [Kitchen 2003](../reference/sources.md#kitchen); [Thompson 1974](../reference/sources.md#thompson-patriarchs); [Van Seters 1975](../reference/sources.md#van-seters); [Finkelstein & Silberman 2001](../reference/sources.md#finkelstein-silberman); [Miller & Hayes 2006](../reference/sources.md#miller-hayes).

<a id="sojourn"></a>

## Israel in Egypt: c. 1876–1446 BC, or a shorter stay ending c. 1260 BC

Exodus 12:40 gives 430 years in Egypt (the Septuagint counts that from Abraham, about 215 years in Egypt). Joseph is often placed in the Hyksos period (c. 1650–1550 BC) or, on the longer chronology, in the Middle Kingdom.

Semitic groups living in the eastern Nile Delta are well attested in the Middle
Bronze Age, and the Hyksos (Asiatic rulers of Lower Egypt, c. 1650–1550 BC) are
often linked with Joseph's rise. "Raamses" (Exod 1:11), the store city the
Israelites built, is best known as Pi-Ramesses, built by Ramesses II
(1279–1213 BC), which supports the late exodus date; supporters of the early
date read the name as an updating by a later scribe (as in Gen 47:11).

**Sources:** [Hoffmeier 1997](../reference/sources.md#hoffmeier); [Kitchen 2003](../reference/sources.md#kitchen); [Miller & Hayes 2006](../reference/sources.md#miller-hayes).

<a id="exodus"></a>

## The exodus: c. 1446 BC (early) or c. 1260 BC (late)

1 Kings 6:1 (480 years before Solomon's 4th year, 967/966 BC) gives c. 1446 BC. The city Raamses (Exod 1:11) and the archaeology of the 13th century point to c. 1260 BC under Ramesses II (Kitchen, Hoffmeier). By 1208 BC the Merneptah Stele knows Israel as a people in Canaan.

**Early date, c. 1446 BC.** Taken literally, 1 Kings 6:1 and Judges 11:26 (300
years between the conquest and Jephthah) fit a 15th-century exodus under
Thutmose III or Amenhotep II. Supporters cite Bryant Wood's reading of
Jericho's destruction (c. 1400 BC).

**Late date, c. 1260 BC.** The store city Raamses; destruction layers at several
Canaanite cities in the late 13th century; the appearance of hundreds of small
highland villages c. 1200 BC; the Merneptah Stele (1208 BC). On this view the
480 years is a schematic figure (12 generations × 40). Held by Kitchen and
Hoffmeier, among others.

**Critical views.** Many historians find no archaeological trace of a large
exodus or desert wandering. They see Israel emerging mainly from within Canaan
(Dever; Finkelstein and Silberman), with the exodus story preserving the memory
of a smaller group and later shaped as national epic.

In the chapter headings, the two dates appear as *Early exodus* and *Late
exodus*; the critical view is noted where relevant.

**Sources:** [Kitchen 2003](../reference/sources.md#kitchen); [Hoffmeier 1997](../reference/sources.md#hoffmeier); [Wood 1990](../reference/sources.md#wood-jericho); [Merneptah Stele](../reference/sources.md#merneptah-stele); [Dever 2003](../reference/sources.md#dever); [Finkelstein & Silberman 2001](../reference/sources.md#finkelstein-silberman); [Miller & Hayes 2006](../reference/sources.md#miller-hayes).

<a id="wilderness"></a>

## Sinai and the wilderness years: 1446–1406 BC (early) or c. 1260–1220 BC (late)

Israel reaches Sinai in the third month after leaving Egypt (Exod 19:1); the tabernacle is set up a year after the exodus (Exod 40:17); the census of Numbers 1 is a month later; the forty years end on the plains of Moab (Deut 1:3). Absolute dates follow the exodus date.

The internal dates of Exodus–Deuteronomy are precise relative to the exodus
(year 1, month 3; year 2, month 1, day 1; year 40, month 11, day 1), so each
chapter can be placed within the forty years even though the starting year is
disputed.

**Sources:** [Kitchen 2003](../reference/sources.md#kitchen); [Miller & Hayes 2006](../reference/sources.md#miller-hayes).

<a id="conquest"></a>

## Entry into Canaan: c. 1406 BC (early) or c. 1220 BC (late)

Forty years after the exodus: c. 1406 BC on the early date, c. 1220 BC on the late. Archaeology has not confirmed a single campaign; Jericho and Ai are much debated.

Kenyon's excavations dated Jericho's fall to the end of the Middle Bronze Age
(c. 1550 BC), too early for either date; Wood argued for c. 1400 BC. Ai seems
to have been unoccupied in the Late Bronze Age. Hazor was burned in the 13th
century. Many historians read Joshua's sweeping conquest as a later, idealized
account; Judges 1 itself shows a slower, partial settlement.

**Sources:** [Kenyon 1957](../reference/sources.md#kenyon-jericho); [Wood 1990](../reference/sources.md#wood-jericho); [Mazar 1990](../reference/sources.md#mazar); [Dever 2003](../reference/sources.md#dever); [Finkelstein & Silberman 2001](../reference/sources.md#finkelstein-silberman).

<a id="judges-period"></a>

## The time of the judges: c. 1380–1050 BC (early) or c. 1200–1050 BC (late)

Between Joshua and Saul. The years given in Judges add up to about 410, too many for either exodus date unless some judges overlapped, as most scholars assume. Late Bronze to early Iron Age.

The stories are arranged as a cycle, not a strict sequence, and several judges
were probably local leaders at the same time. Jephthah's "300 years" (Judg
11:26) is used for the early date. The Philistines, prominent in Samson's
stories, settled on the coast c. 1175 BC.

**Sources:** [Kitchen 2003](../reference/sources.md#kitchen); [Miller & Hayes 2006](../reference/sources.md#miller-hayes); [Provan, Long & Longman 2003](../reference/sources.md#provan-long-longman).

<a id="saul"></a>

## Saul: c. 1050–1010 BC

The text of 1 Samuel 13:1 giving Saul's age and reign is damaged; Acts 13:21 gives 40 years. Most date his reign c. 1050/1030–1010 BC, counting back from David.

1 Samuel 13:1 reads literally "Saul was a year old when he began to reign, and
he reigned two years over Israel", where numbers have dropped out. Estimates of
his reign range from about 2 to 40 years.

**Sources:** [McCarter 1980](../reference/sources.md#mccarter-sam); [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Miller & Hayes 2006](../reference/sources.md#miller-hayes).

<a id="david"></a>

## David: c. 1010–970 BC

Forty years: 7½ at Hebron and 33 at Jerusalem (2 Sam 5:4–5), counted back from Solomon. The Tel Dan inscription (9th century BC) names "the house of David".

The dates come from counting back from the division of the kingdom (931/930 BC)
through Solomon's 40 years. The Tel Dan stele confirms David as the founder of
Judah's dynasty. How large his kingdom was is debated: Finkelstein argues that
10th-century Jerusalem was a small highland town, while others (Mazar) point
to monumental building.

**Sources:** [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Tel Dan inscription](../reference/sources.md#tel-dan); [Finkelstein & Silberman 2001](../reference/sources.md#finkelstein-silberman); [Mazar 1990](../reference/sources.md#mazar); [Provan, Long & Longman 2003](../reference/sources.md#provan-long-longman).

<a id="solomon"></a>

## Solomon: c. 970–931 BC

Forty years (1 Kgs 11:42). The temple was begun in his 4th year (967/966 BC, 1 Kgs 6:1) and finished seven years later (6:38).

Thiele dates the accession to 971/970 BC. The temple dates in 1 Kings 6 are the
anchor for the early exodus date. Shoshenq I's campaign five years after
Solomon's death (1 Kgs 14:25) gives an Egyptian synchronism c. 925 BC.

**Sources:** [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Galil 1996](../reference/sources.md#galil); [Shoshenq I relief](../reference/sources.md#shoshenq-relief); [Cogan; Cogan & Tadmor](../reference/sources.md#cogan-kings).

<a id="schism"></a>

## The divided kingdom: 931/930 BC

After Solomon's death the northern tribes made Jeroboam I king (1 Kgs 12). Shishak (Shoshenq I) invaded in Rehoboam's 5th year, c. 925 BC (1 Kgs 14:25), attested by his relief at Karnak.

From here on, Kings dates each reign by the other kingdom's (e.g. "in the 18th
year of Jeroboam, Abijam began to reign", 1 Kgs 15:1). The figures don't add up
simply; Thiele resolved them with co-regencies and different ways of counting
accession years, and most later chronologies (Galil, Cogan and Tadmor) refine
his.

**Sources:** [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Galil 1996](../reference/sources.md#galil); [Shoshenq I relief](../reference/sources.md#shoshenq-relief).

<a id="ahab-qarqar"></a>

## Omri's dynasty and the battle of Qarqar: 885–841 BC (Qarqar 853 BC)

Ahab fought Assyria at Qarqar in 853 BC (Kurkh Monolith), shortly before his death. Mesha of Moab's revolt after Ahab's death (2 Kgs 3) is told on the Mesha Stele.

Shalmaneser III's annals list "Ahab the Israelite" with 2,000 chariots at
Qarqar in his 6th year, 853 BC. Twelve years later (841 BC) Jehu paid him
tribute. Fitting Ahab's death and Jehu's accession into those 12 years is the
keystone of Thiele's chronology.

**Sources:** [Kurkh Monolith](../reference/sources.md#kurkh-monolith); [Mesha Stele](../reference/sources.md#mesha-stele); [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Cogan; Cogan & Tadmor](../reference/sources.md#cogan-kings).

<a id="jehu"></a>

## Jehu's coup: 841 BC

Jehu killed Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah (2 Kgs 9) and paid tribute to Shalmaneser III in 841 BC (Black Obelisk). Athaliah ruled Judah for six years after.

The Black Obelisk shows "Jehu son of Omri" (i.e. king of Omri's land) bowing
before Shalmaneser, the only contemporary picture of an Israelite king. The Tel
Dan stele seems to have Hazael claiming the killing of Joram and Ahaziah.

**Sources:** [Black Obelisk](../reference/sources.md#black-obelisk); [Tel Dan inscription](../reference/sources.md#tel-dan); [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele).

<a id="eighth-century"></a>

## Jeroboam II, Uzziah and the Assyrian advance: c. 793–722 BC

Jeroboam II (793–753) and Uzziah (792–740) ruled in prosperity; Tiglath-pileser III (745–727) then pushed west, Israel and Aram attacked Judah (the Syro-Ephraimite war, 734–732) and Damascus fell in 732. The eclipse of 763 BC in the Assyrian eponym list fixes these dates.

The Assyrian eponym list, tied to the solar eclipse of 15 June 763 BC, gives
year-by-year dates for Tiglath-pileser's campaigns, which name Menahem, Pekah,
Hoshea and Ahaz (Jehoahaz) of Judah. Amos and Hosea prophesied under Jeroboam
II; Isaiah began "in the year King Uzziah died" (Isa 6:1, c. 740/739 BC).

**Sources:** [Assyrian eponym list](../reference/sources.md#eponym-canon); [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Cogan; Cogan & Tadmor](../reference/sources.md#cogan-kings); [ANET](../reference/sources.md#anet).

<a id="samaria-fall"></a>

## Fall of Samaria: 722/720 BC

After a three-year siege Samaria fell to Shalmaneser V in 722 BC; Sargon II (722–705) claims its capture and deported its people (2 Kgs 17).

Babylonian and Assyrian sources credit Shalmaneser V with the capture in 722;
Sargon, his successor, claims it in his annals and resettled the region c. 720.
2 Kings 18:10 synchronizes the fall with Hezekiah's 6th year, one of the hardest
points in the chronology of Judah (see *Hezekiah and Sennacherib*).

**Sources:** [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Cogan; Cogan & Tadmor](../reference/sources.md#cogan-kings); [ANET](../reference/sources.md#anet).

<a id="hezekiah-701"></a>

## Hezekiah and Sennacherib: Hezekiah 716/715–687/686 BC; invasion 701 BC

Sennacherib invaded Judah in 701 BC, "Hezekiah's 14th year" (2 Kgs 18:13), which gives Hezekiah's accession in 716/715 BC (Thiele). His annals describe shutting Hezekiah in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage".

The 14th-year synchronism (701 BC) conflicts with 2 Kings 18:1, 9–10, which put
Hezekiah on the throne before Samaria fell (722). Thiele kept 716/715 and called
the other figures a later scribe's miscalculation; others propose a co-regency
with Ahaz from c. 729 BC (Galil) or emend the 14th year to the 24th. The Siloam
tunnel and the Lachish reliefs belong to this period.

**Sources:** [Sennacherib's annals](../reference/sources.md#sennacherib-annals); [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Galil 1996](../reference/sources.md#galil); [Cogan; Cogan & Tadmor](../reference/sources.md#cogan-kings).

<a id="josiah"></a>

## Josiah and the book of the law: 640–609 BC (reform 622 BC)

Josiah reigned 31 years. In his 18th year (622 BC) the "book of the law" was found in the temple (2 Kgs 22:8), widely identified with Deuteronomy. He was killed at Megiddo by Pharaoh Necho in 609 BC.

Since de Wette (1805) and Wellhausen, critical scholars have linked the book
found in 622 with Deuteronomy, either written then or not long before. Necho's
march to help Assyria at Harran in 609 is in the Babylonian Chronicle. Jeremiah
began in Josiah's 13th year (627 BC, Jer 1:2).

**Sources:** [Wellhausen 1883](../reference/sources.md#wellhausen); [Babylonian Chronicle](../reference/sources.md#babylonian-chronicle); [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Cogan; Cogan & Tadmor](../reference/sources.md#cogan-kings).

<a id="babylon-597-587"></a>

## Nebuchadnezzar and the fall of Jerusalem: 605–586 BC

Nebuchadnezzar beat Egypt at Carchemish in 605 BC, captured Jerusalem on 2 Adar (15/16 March) 597 BC and exiled Jehoiachin (Babylonian Chronicle), then destroyed the city and temple in Zedekiah's 11th year: 587 or 586 BC.

**597 BC** is fixed to the day by the Babylonian Chronicle. **The destruction**
(2 Kgs 25:8, the 19th year of Nebuchadnezzar) is dated to summer 587 BC by many
(e.g. Galil, Cogan and Tadmor) and to 586 BC by Thiele and others. It depends on
whether Judah counted regnal years from spring or autumn and how the Bible
counted Nebuchadnezzar's years.

**Sources:** [Babylonian Chronicle](../reference/sources.md#babylonian-chronicle); [Grayson 1975](../reference/sources.md#grayson-abc); [Parker & Dubberstein 1956](../reference/sources.md#parker-dubberstein); [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Galil 1996](../reference/sources.md#galil); [Cogan; Cogan & Tadmor](../reference/sources.md#cogan-kings).

<a id="exile"></a>

## The Babylonian exile: 597/587–539 BC

From the deportations of 597 and 587/586 BC to Cyrus's capture of Babylon in 539 BC. Jehoiachin was released by Evil-Merodach (Amel-Marduk) in 562 BC (2 Kgs 25:27). Ezekiel dates his oracles from Jehoiachin's exile (597 BC).

Babylonian ration tablets list "Yaukin, king of the land of Yahud" receiving
oil in Babylon, confirming Jehoiachin's captivity. The "seventy years" of
Jeremiah 25:11 and 29:10 are counted from 605 or 587 BC, to 539 or 515 BC.

**Sources:** [Parker & Dubberstein 1956](../reference/sources.md#parker-dubberstein); [Grayson 1975](../reference/sources.md#grayson-abc); [ANET](../reference/sources.md#anet); [Zimmerli 1979–83](../reference/sources.md#zimmerli).

<a id="return"></a>

## Cyrus, the return and the second temple: 539–515 BC

Cyrus took Babylon in October 539 BC; his decree (Ezra 1:1–4) is dated to his first year, 538 BC. Rebuilding resumed in Darius I's 2nd year, 520 BC (Haggai, Zechariah), and the temple was finished on 3 Adar of his 6th year, March 515 BC (Ezra 6:15).

The Cyrus Cylinder describes his policy of returning deported peoples and their
gods, which fits the decree in Ezra 1. Haggai's and Zechariah's dates convert
exactly with Babylonian calendars (e.g. Hag 1:1, 1 Elul year 2 = 29 August 520
BC).

**Sources:** [Cyrus Cylinder](../reference/sources.md#cyrus-cylinder); [Parker & Dubberstein 1956](../reference/sources.md#parker-dubberstein); [Meyers & Meyers 1987](../reference/sources.md#meyers-haggai); [Williamson 1985](../reference/sources.md#williamson-ezra).

<a id="ezra-nehemiah"></a>

## Ezra and Nehemiah: Ezra 458 BC (or 398 BC); Nehemiah 445–c. 430 BC

Nehemiah came in the 20th year of Artaxerxes I, 445 BC (Neh 2:1), and went back in his 32nd, 433 BC (13:6). Ezra came in "the 7th year of Artaxerxes" (Ezra 7:7): 458 BC if Artaxerxes I, the traditional order; 398 BC if Artaxerxes II, which would put Ezra after Nehemiah.

The Elephantine papyri (407 BC) name the sons of Sanballat, Nehemiah's opponent,
as active, confirming Nehemiah under Artaxerxes I. For Ezra, 458 BC is
defended by Williamson and many others; 398 BC (first argued by Van Hoonacker)
is still held by some, and a compromise emends Ezra 7:7 to the 37th year
(428 BC).

**Sources:** [Elephantine papyri](../reference/sources.md#elephantine-papyri); [Williamson 1985](../reference/sources.md#williamson-ezra); [Parker & Dubberstein 1956](../reference/sources.md#parker-dubberstein); [Miller & Hayes 2006](../reference/sources.md#miller-hayes).

<a id="xerxes"></a>

## Xerxes I (Ahasuerus): 486–465 BC

Ahasuerus of Esther is Xerxes I. The book dates its events to his 3rd year (483 BC, Esth 1:3), 7th year (479 BC, 2:16) and 12th year (474 BC, 3:7).

The Hebrew name *Ahashwerosh* matches Persian *Khshayarsha* (Xerxes). How far
the story is historical is debated: it fits Persian court customs well, but its
queen Vashti and Esther are unknown outside the Bible, and Herodotus names
Xerxes' queen as Amestris.

**Sources:** [Fox 1991](../reference/sources.md#fox-esther); [Parker & Dubberstein 1956](../reference/sources.md#parker-dubberstein).

<a id="antiochus"></a>

## Antiochus IV and the Maccabean crisis: 175–164 BC (temple desecrated 167 BC)

Antiochus IV desecrated the temple on 15 Kislev 167 BC (1 Macc 1:54); Judas Maccabeus rededicated it on 25 Kislev 164 BC. Most scholars date the final form of Daniel to 167–164 BC, because Daniel 11 tracks these events closely up to about 165 BC.

Daniel 11:21–39 matches Antiochus's career in detail, while 11:40–45
(his death between the sea and Jerusalem) does not match his actual death in
Persia in late 164 BC. Critical scholars take this as the point where the
author's own time ends (Collins). Conservative scholars take Daniel as written
by Daniel in the 6th century BC and chs. 8 and 11 as predictive prophecy.

**Sources:** [1 Maccabees](../reference/sources.md#maccabees-1); [Collins 1993](../reference/sources.md#collins-daniel); [Dillard & Longman](../reference/sources.md#dillard-longman); [Dead Sea Scrolls](../reference/sources.md#dead-sea-scrolls).

<a id="pentateuch"></a>

## Composition of the Pentateuch: Mosaic (c. 1400/1250 BC) or compiled c. 950–400 BC

Jewish and Christian tradition ascribes Genesis–Deuteronomy to Moses. Most critical scholars see it compiled from sources over centuries: older traditions written down in the monarchy, Deuteronomy linked with 622 BC, and priestly material (P) from the exile or after, completed by c. 400 BC.

**Tradition.** Moses as author (Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.39; Talmud, *Bava
Batra* 14b), with Deuteronomy 34 (his death) added by Joshua. Many conservative
scholars hold that Moses wrote or supplied most of it, with later editing (e.g.
place names updated; Gen 36:31 "before any king reigned over Israel").

**Documentary hypothesis** (Wellhausen 1883): four sources, J (c. 950–850), E
(c. 850–750), D (7th century) and P (6th–5th century). Since the 1970s many
scholars have abandoned J and E as separate documents in favour of a
non-priestly and a priestly layer, with most of the writing done in the exile
and Persian period. Milgrom and others date much of P before the exile.
Ezra's reading of "the book of the law of Moses" (Neh 8, c. 458/445 BC) is often
taken as the point by which it was complete.

**Sources:** [Wellhausen 1883](../reference/sources.md#wellhausen); [Josephus, Against Apion, 1.37–43](../reference/sources.md#josephus-apion); [Talmud, Bava Batra, 14b–15a](../reference/sources.md#bava-batra); [Milgrom 1991](../reference/sources.md#milgrom-lev); [Collins, Hebrew Bible](../reference/sources.md#collins-intro); [Dillard & Longman](../reference/sources.md#dillard-longman); [Kitchen 2003](../reference/sources.md#kitchen).

<a id="dtr-history"></a>

## Composition of Joshua to Kings: c. 620–550 BC (final form after 562 BC)

Joshua, Judges, Samuel and Kings share Deuteronomy's language and outlook. Noth (1943) saw one exilic author (after 562 BC, the last event in Kings); Cross (1973) a first edition under Josiah (c. 620 BC) revised in the exile. Tradition names Joshua, Samuel, and Jeremiah as authors.

Kings ends with Jehoiachin's release in 562 BC (2 Kgs 25:27–30), so its final
form is no earlier. The books draw on older sources they name, such as "the
book of the chronicles of the kings of Israel" and "the book of Jashar". The
Talmud (*Bava Batra* 15a) ascribes Joshua to Joshua, Judges and Samuel to
Samuel, and Kings to Jeremiah.

**Sources:** [Noth 1943](../reference/sources.md#noth-dtr); [Cross 1973](../reference/sources.md#cross); [Talmud, Bava Batra, 14b–15a](../reference/sources.md#bava-batra); [Collins, Hebrew Bible](../reference/sources.md#collins-intro); [Schniedewind 2004](../reference/sources.md#schniedewind).

<a id="chronicler"></a>

## Composition of Chronicles: c. 400–250 BC

Chronicles ends with Cyrus's decree (539/538 BC) and traces David's line about six generations past Zerubbabel (1 Chr 3:19–24), so most date it to the late Persian or early Greek period. Tradition names Ezra.

Chronicles uses Samuel and Kings as a source, and coins named "darics" (Persian,
first minted under Darius I) appear in 1 Chr 29:7. Japhet dates it to the late
4th century BC. Older scholarship treated Chronicles and Ezra–Nehemiah as one
work; Japhet and Williamson argued that they are separate.

**Sources:** [Japhet 1993](../reference/sources.md#japhet); [Knoppers 2004](../reference/sources.md#knoppers-chron); [Williamson 1985](../reference/sources.md#williamson-ezra); [Talmud, Bava Batra, 14b–15a](../reference/sources.md#bava-batra).

<a id="psalter"></a>

## Composition of the Psalms: c. 1000–300 BC

The psalms were written over centuries: 73 carry the heading "of David" (c. 1000 BC), one "of Moses" (Ps 90), others are plainly exilic (Ps 137) or later. The five-book collection was complete by the 2nd century BC, judging by the Dead Sea Scrolls.

The headings (*le-David*, "of/for/to David") are old but probably not
original; many scholars read them as attributions or dedications, not claims of
authorship. Royal psalms (2, 45, 72, 110) need a king and so come from the
monarchy; laments over the temple's destruction (74, 79, 137) come after 587 BC.
The Qumran Psalms scrolls show the order of Books IV–V was still fluid in the
1st century BC.

**Sources:** [Kraus 1988–89](../reference/sources.md#kraus-psalms); [Dead Sea Scrolls](../reference/sources.md#dead-sea-scrolls); [Collins, Hebrew Bible](../reference/sources.md#collins-intro); [Dillard & Longman](../reference/sources.md#dillard-longman).

<a id="prophets-isaiah-parts"></a>

## The three parts of Isaiah: Isaiah c. 740–700 BC; or chs. 40–55 c. 550–539 BC and 56–66 c. 538–450 BC

Tradition takes the whole book as the work of Isaiah son of Amoz (c. 740–700 BC). Since Duhm (1892) most critical scholars divide it into First Isaiah (much of chs. 1–39, 8th century, with later additions), Second Isaiah (40–55, written among the exiles c. 550–539 BC; Cyrus is named in 44:28 and 45:1) and Third Isaiah (56–66, Judah after the return, c. 538–450 BC).

**One author.** The heading (1:1) names only Isaiah; Sirach (c. 180 BC) credits
Isaiah with comforting "those who mourned in Zion" and foretelling "what was to
be" (Sir 48:24–25), language from chs. 40–66; the great Isaiah scroll from
Qumran (1QIsa^a, 2nd century BC) runs ch. 39 straight into ch. 40; and the New
Testament quotes chs. 40–66 under Isaiah's name. Conservative scholars (Oswalt)
take chs. 40–66 as Isaiah's own prophecy, written late in his life for a future
exile, and Cyrus's naming as predictive.

**Two or three Isaiahs.** Chapters 40–55 address exiles in Babylon as their
present audience, speak of Jerusalem as already ruined (44:26; 51:3), and name
Cyrus as the coming deliverer, so most scholars date them to Cyrus's rise:
after his defeat of Media (c. 550 BC) and before he took Babylon (539 BC).
Chapters 56–66 assume people back in Judah and quarrels in the community; most
date them between the return (538) and Nehemiah's time, some to before the
temple was finished in 515 BC (63:18; 64:10–11; 66:1). Within chs. 1–39, the
oracles on Babylon (13–14), the "Isaiah apocalypse" (24–27) and chs. 34–35 are
widely dated to the exile or later. Blenkinsopp's commentary follows this
division; Childs reads the parts as one canonical book whatever their dates.

**In the headings.** Chapters 40–66, and the disputed parts of 1–39, give both
dates ("Isaiah" and the critical date), with a range spanning both.

**Sources:** [Oswalt 1986–98](../reference/sources.md#oswalt-isaiah); [Blenkinsopp 2000–03](../reference/sources.md#blenkinsopp-isaiah); [Childs 1979](../reference/sources.md#childs); [Sirach, 48:22–25](../reference/sources.md#sirach); [Dead Sea Scrolls](../reference/sources.md#dead-sea-scrolls); [Collins, Hebrew Bible](../reference/sources.md#collins-intro); [Dillard & Longman](../reference/sources.md#dillard-longman).

<a id="prophets-judah-last-kings"></a>

## Judah's last kings (Jeremiah's regnal dates): 609–587/586 BC

Jehoahaz (Shallum) ruled three months in 609 BC; Jehoiakim 609–598; Jehoiachin (Jeconiah, Coniah) three months to 2 Adar (16 March) 597; Zedekiah 597 to the fall of Jerusalem in 587 or 586. Jehoiakim's 4th year = 605/604 (Jer 25:1; 46:2); Zedekiah's 4th year = 594/593 (28:1; 51:59).

Jeremiah dates his oracles by the kings of Judah, and twice also by
Nebuchadnezzar: Jehoiakim's 4th year was Nebuchadnezzar's 1st (25:1), the year
of Carchemish (46:2; Babylonian Chronicle, summer 605 BC), and Zedekiah's 10th
was Nebuchadnezzar's 18th (32:1), 588/587 BC. The siege of Jerusalem began on
10/X of Zedekiah's 9th year (Jer 52:4; 2 Kgs 25:1), January 588 BC, and the wall
was breached on 9/IV of his 11th year (Jer 39:2; 52:6–7), July 587 BC on the
reckoning of Galil and Cogan & Tadmor, July 586 BC on Thiele's. The Babylonian
Chronicle fixes Jehoiachin's surrender to the day (2 Adar, year 7). Whether
Judah counted regnal years from spring (Nisan) or autumn (Tishri) moves several
of these dates by a year, so a heading such as "594/593 BC" means the regnal
year that straddles them.

**Sources:** [Babylonian Chronicle](../reference/sources.md#babylonian-chronicle); [Grayson 1975](../reference/sources.md#grayson-abc); [Parker & Dubberstein 1956](../reference/sources.md#parker-dubberstein); [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Galil 1996](../reference/sources.md#galil); [Cogan; Cogan & Tadmor](../reference/sources.md#cogan-kings); [Lundbom 1999–2004](../reference/sources.md#lundbom-jer).

<a id="prophets-ezekiel-dates"></a>

## Ezekiel's dated oracles: 593–571 BC

Ezekiel dates thirteen oracles by year, month and day "of King Jehoiachin's captivity" (1:2; 33:21; 40:1), counted from the spring of 597 BC. Converted with Parker & Dubberstein's tables, they run from 31 July 593 BC (1:1–2) to 26 April 571 BC (29:17), the latest.

Jehoiachin surrendered on 2 Adar 597 BC (Babylonian Chronicle), so year 1 of
the captivity is the year beginning Nisan 597 BC, and year *n* begins in the
spring of 597 − (n − 1) BC. Months are counted from Nisan (I) in spring. The
usual conversions (Parker & Dubberstein, as tabulated in Zimmerli's
commentary) are:

| Text | Year/month/day | Date |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1–2 | 5/IV/5 | 31 July 593 BC |
| 8:1 | 6/VI/5 | 17 September 592 BC |
| 20:1 | 7/V/10 | 14 August 591 BC |
| 24:1 | 9/X/10 | 15 January 588 BC |
| 26:1 | 11/?/1 | 587/586 BC (month missing) |
| 29:1 | 10/X/12 | 7 January 587 BC |
| 29:17 | 27/I/1 | 26 April 571 BC |
| 30:20 | 11/I/7 | 29 April 587 BC |
| 31:1 | 11/III/1 | 21 June 587 BC |
| 32:1 | 12/XII/1 | 3 March 585 BC |
| 32:17 | 12/?/15 | 585 BC (month missing; Greek "first month": April 586) |
| 33:21 | 12/X/5 | 8 January 585 BC (some manuscripts "11th year": January 586) |
| 40:1 | 25/I?/10 | 28 April 573 BC (or October 574 if the year began in autumn) |

Ezekiel 40:1 equates the 25th year of captivity with "the fourteenth year after
the city was struck", which fits a fall of Jerusalem in 587 BC. The news of the
fall reaching Babylon in the 10th month of the 12th year (33:21) means a journey
of about five months if the city fell in 586, or eighteen months if it fell in
587, which is why some prefer the "11th year" reading there. The "thirtieth
year" of 1:1 is unexplained: often Ezekiel's age (so Origen), or 30 years from
Josiah's reform of 622 BC, which also lands on 593.

<a id="twelve-earthquake"></a>

## The earthquake in Uzziah's reign: c. 760 BC

Amos prophesied "two years before the earthquake" (Amos 1:1), under Uzziah and Jeroboam II. The quake was still remembered more than two centuries later ("as you fled from before the earthquake in the days of Uzziah", Zech 14:5). Excavators date a major earthquake destruction at Hazor and other sites to the mid-8th century BC, usually c. 760 BC.

**Biblical limits.** Amos names Uzziah (792–740 BC on Thiele's dates, sole reign
from 767) and Jeroboam II (793–753), so his ministry falls before 753. The
phrase "two years before the earthquake" treats the quake as a well-known
reference point, and suggests that Amos's preaching was brief.

**Later memory.** Zechariah 14:5 still uses it as a byword. Josephus links the
earthquake with Uzziah's attempt to burn incense in the temple (2 Chr 26:16–21),
a tradition the Bible itself does not make.

**Archaeology.** Destruction by earthquake in the mid-8th century BC has been
identified at Hazor (Stratum VI) and several other sites in Israel and Judah.
The date c. 760 BC is the usual estimate; it cannot be fixed to the year.

**Sources:** [Thiele 1983](../reference/sources.md#thiele); [Wolff 1977](../reference/sources.md#wolff-joel-amos); [Josephus, Antiquities, 9.222–227](../reference/sources.md#josephus-ant); [Mazar 1990](../reference/sources.md#mazar).

<a id="twelve-nineveh"></a>

## Thebes (663 BC) and the fall of Nineveh (612 BC): 663–612 BC

Nahum 3:8–10 recalls the sack of Thebes ("No-Amon") by Ashurbanipal of Assyria in 663 BC as past; Nahum and Zephaniah 2:13–15 foretell Nineveh's fall, which came in 612 BC to the Babylonians and Medes (Babylonian Chronicle). Oracles against Nineveh as a living power fall between the two dates.

**Thebes, 663 BC.** Ashurbanipal's annals describe his second Egyptian campaign,
in which Thebes was taken and plundered after the flight of the Kushite king
Tantamani. Nahum uses its fate as a warning to Nineveh: a great river city that
fell anyway.

**Nineveh, 612 BC.** The Babylonian chronicle of the fall of Nineveh records
that Nabopolassar of Babylon and Cyaxares the Mede besieged the city and took
it in the summer of Nabopolassar's 14th year, 612 BC. Assyrian resistance went
on at Harran until 610–609 BC.

**Assyria's decline.** After Ashurbanipal's last years (died c. 631 BC) Assyria
lost Babylonia (626 BC) and declined rapidly. Oracles that treat Assyria as still
oppressive but doomed are often placed in the 620s and 610s; those that stress
its power, closer to 663.

**Sources:** [ANET](../reference/sources.md#anet); [Grayson 1975](../reference/sources.md#grayson-abc); [Babylonian Chronicle](../reference/sources.md#babylonian-chronicle); [Roberts 1991](../reference/sources.md#roberts-nahum).

<a id="twelve-darius-dates"></a>

## The dated oracles of Haggai and Zechariah: 29 August 520 – 7 December 518 BC

Haggai and Zechariah 1–8 date their oracles by the year of Darius I (522–486 BC), month and day. Converted with Parker & Dubberstein's tables they run from 1 Elul of Darius's 2nd year (29 August 520 BC, Hag 1:1) to 4 Kislev of his 4th year (7 December 518 BC, Zech 7:1).

Darius I took the throne in 522 BC; his first regnal year began in Nisan 521, so
his 2nd year ran from spring 520 to spring 519 BC. Months are counted from the
spring (Nisan = month 1), as in the Babylonian calendar.

| Reference | Text date | Julian date |
|---|---|---|
| Hag 1:1 | year 2, month 6, day 1 | 29 August 520 BC |
| Hag 1:15 | year 2, month 6, day 24 | 21 September 520 BC |
| Hag 2:1 | year 2, month 7, day 21 | 17 October 520 BC |
| Zech 1:1 | year 2, month 8 (no day) | October–November 520 BC |
| Hag 2:10, 2:18, 2:20 | year 2, month 9, day 24 | 18 December 520 BC |
| Zech 1:7 | year 2, month 11 (Shebat), day 24 | 15 February 519 BC |
| Zech 7:1 | year 4, month 9 (Kislev), day 4 | 7 December 518 BC |

The temple was finished on 3 Adar of Darius's 6th year, March 515 BC
(Ezra 6:15). Ezra 5:1 and 6:14 name Haggai and Zechariah as the prophets who
urged the rebuilding.

**Sources:** [Parker & Dubberstein 1956](../reference/sources.md#parker-dubberstein); [Meyers & Meyers 1987](../reference/sources.md#meyers-haggai).

<a id="twelve-deutero-zechariah"></a>

## The date of Zechariah 9–14: c. 520–470 BC (Zechariah) or 5th–4th century BC (later prophets)

Zechariah 9–14 has no dates, no mention of Zechariah, Joshua, Zerubbabel or the temple building, and a different style from chs. 1–8. Tradition reads it as Zechariah's later oracles; most critical scholars assign it to one or more anonymous prophets of the later Persian or early Greek period.

**Two "burdens".** Chapters 9–11 and 12–14 each open with *massa*, "burden" or
"oracle" (WEB "A revelation", 9:1; 12:1), as does Malachi (1:1). Many scholars treat the three as
an appendix of anonymous collections added at the end of the Twelve.

**Late date.** The sons of Zion are set against "your sons, Greece" (9:13);
9:1–8 traces a conqueror's route down the Syrian and Phoenician coast, which
some have linked with Alexander's campaign of 332 BC; the shepherd allegories
(11:4–17; 13:7–9) suggest conflicts over leadership in the restored community.
Most critical commentators put the chapters in the 5th or 4th century BC.

**Early date.** Older critics, starting in the 17th–18th centuries, put chs.
9–11 before the exile because they mention Ephraim, Assyria and Egypt as live
powers (9:10, 13; 10:10–11); Matthew 27:9–10 quotes 11:12–13 under Jeremiah's
name. This view is now rarely held.

**Traditional view.** Conservative scholars ascribe the chapters to Zechariah
himself, late in a long ministry (after 480 BC), the change of style reflecting
a change of subject from the temple to the distant future; Greece was already a
known power after Darius's and Xerxes' wars.

**Sources:** [Petersen 1995](../reference/sources.md#petersen-zech); [Meyers & Meyers 1987](../reference/sources.md#meyers-haggai); [Childs 1979](../reference/sources.md#childs); [Collins, Hebrew Bible](../reference/sources.md#collins-intro); [Dillard & Longman](../reference/sources.md#dillard-longman).
